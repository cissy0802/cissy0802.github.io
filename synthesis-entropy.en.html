Entropy: One Word, Five Rooms

BigCat · 2026-07 · Cross-Site Synthesis
Across this hub, the word "entropy" turns up on at least six unrelated sites: physics counts microscopic arrangements, mathematics measures uncertainty, the AI site trains models by minimising it, meta-knowledge calls life an anti-entropy machine, and a deep-reading page says the default state of consciousness is psychic entropy. They use the same word, the same symbol, sometimes literally the same formula. None of that means they are talking about the same thing. This piece does two jobs. First it locates the mathematical spine these usages genuinely share — which is harder and more real than most people assume. Then it marks the exact vertebra where that spine snaps, and offers a test: what does something have to satisfy before it earns the name entropy?

1. Five Rooms

Before any definitions, stand inside five rooms.

The first is a laboratory. A cup of warm water sits on the bench; heat flows from water to air and never the other way. Write out Newton's equations, Maxwell's equations, Schrödinger's equation, and you find that not one of them distinguishes a direction of time — swap t for −t and every equation still holds. Yet cups shatter and never reassemble. In all of physics, exactly one law knows which way time points.

The second is Bell Labs in 1948. Shannon is asking a purely engineering question: what is the minimum number of bits needed to send a message down a wire without loss? He writes down a formula to quantify uncertainty — and discovers, to his surprise, that he has written the same thing Boltzmann had carved on his tombstone seventy years earlier.

The third is a GPU mid-training run. The loss on screen falls from 10.8 to 2.3. That number has units and a physical meaning: it is how many bits, on average, it costs to encode real text using the model's picture of the world. Here entropy is not a quantity being observed. It is a quantity being optimised.

The fourth is a cell. It assembles scattered atoms into membranes, proteins, DNA — order rising against the current, apparently in defiance of the second law. It is not. It simply dumps a larger quantity of disorder into its surroundings.

The fifth is somebody's mind. He has just come back from a week of doing nothing and feels worse than he does at work: unresolved worries flood in and fight with his goals, and his attention is churned into a mess. Psychologists gave that state a name — psychic entropy.

Five rooms, one word. The question is not whether they are connected — they are, more deeply than outsiders suspect. The question is where the connection stops. One step past that boundary is the birthplace of the most abused concept on the internet.

2. Physics: Entropy Is a Number You Can Count

The first thing to dismantle is the slogan "entropy equals disorder." It is handy, and it is precisely what waved through every abuse that follows.

In 1877 Boltzmann nailed entropy down to a formula, later carved on his headstone:

S = k log W

S is entropy. k is Boltzmann's constant (about 1.38×10⁻²³ joules per kelvin — the exchange rate that converts a raw count into physical entropy units). W is the number of microstates: how many distinct molecule-level arrangements are fully compatible with the macroscopic appearance you actually observe — same temperature, same pressure, same colour. The logarithm compresses numbers like 10-to-the-trillions into something manageable, and has the further virtue that entropies of combined systems simply add.

So entropy is not an adjective. It is an act of counting. A neatly ordered deck of cards admits only a handful of arrangements; the messy-looking ones number astronomically. You will essentially never shuffle your way back to order — not because messiness has some mystical pull, but because the messy arrangements overwhelmingly outnumber the tidy ones.

That is what threads the second law's three faces together. Clausius: heat never flows spontaneously from cold to hot. Kelvin: you cannot convert all the heat from a single reservoir into work with no other change. Boltzmann: the total entropy of an isolated system never decreases. The three are provably equivalent — grant any one and the other two follow.

And here is the point that everything in §7 hangs on: the second law is not an iron rule, it is a probabilistic one. It never says entropy cannot decrease, only that a decrease is absurdly improbable. Warm water spontaneously separating into a hot half and a cold half violates no law of mechanics; it simply has a probability of roughly one in an astronomical number. The Poincaré recurrence theorem even guarantees that, given long enough, a finite closed system returns arbitrarily close to its initial low-entropy state — for a macroscopic cup of gas (some 10²³ molecules), that recurrence time is on the order of 10 to the power of 10²³ years. Write that number out and every atom in the universe would not supply enough ink. It is the sheer scale of 10²³ that hardens a statement about probability into a rule that is, in practice, iron. Hold on to this; §7 uses it as a blade.

The Arrow of Time: the Only Law That Knows Which Way Is Forward

We remember the past and not the future, causes precede effects, we age — every felt sign that time flows traces back to the same arrow: rising entropy. But "there are more disordered states" alone does not produce an arrow. The microscopic laws are time-symmetric, so looking backwards the same argument says entropy should rise in that direction too, giving you a curve that is high at both ends and low in the middle — no direction at all. What actually supplies the arrow is an extra empirical fact: the universe began in an extraordinarily low-entropy state (physicists call this the past hypothesis). Only with that very low starting point does entropy have a single direction to climb. Why that starting point exists remains one of the deepest open problems in physics.

A Fuse to Light Early: Entropy Depends on How Coarsely You Look

Entropy's deepest and most misunderstood property is this: it is not an intrinsic property of an object. Take one cup of water. If you care only about its temperature and volume, its entropy is enormous — countless molecular arrangements correspond to that same temperature. If you could somehow record the position and velocity of every single molecule, then for you that cup occupies exactly one microstate and its entropy is zero. Entropy measures the information you don't have. It is half physics and half a statement about what you have chosen to ignore.

The physicist Carlo Rovelli drives the blade deeper in The Order of Time. He asks: on whose authority was the past's entropy "low"? Who decides what counts as ordered? Return to the deck of cards. A sorted deck looks special only because we happen to care about suits and ranks; to a being that cared about some other property, an entirely different set of arrangements would look special. A system's entropy depends on which microstates you bundle together as equivalent, and that blurring — he calls it coarse-graining — is contributed by the observer, not by the world. From which he draws a vertiginous conclusion: the arrow of time may not live in the universe at all, but between us and the universe. "The passage of time," he writes, arises from our ignorance of the world.

This needs a discount. It is the thermal time hypothesis, Rovelli's own programme with his collaborators, and it is far from settled. Physicists such as David Albert and Sean Carroll push back hard: the universe's initial low entropy is an objective fact and cannot be reduced to a matter of perspective, on pain of circularity — we can form memories because entropy was low, so grounding low entropy in our perspective argues in a circle. Read it as a first-rate physicist's very attractive philosophical conjecture, not as a settled physical result.

But even after that discount, coarse-graining itself is an uncontroversial technical fact: to compute any entropy at all, you must first declare which macroscopic variables you are bundling microstates under. That is not philosophy; it is step one of writing the formula. It becomes the first test in §7.

3. Information: the Same Formula in Different Clothes

In 1948, working on communication, Shannon independently arrived at essentially the same object:

H = −Σ p(x) log₂ p(x)

H is the information entropy of a probability distribution, measured in bits — one bit being the information needed to halve the space of possibilities, the answer to one well-chosen yes/no question. p(x) is the probability of outcome x, and −log₂ p(x) is that outcome's surprisal: the rarer the event, the larger the term. Weighting surprisal by probability and summing makes H the average surprise.

Why must it be a logarithm? Because information has to be additive. Two independent coin flips should carry 1+1 = 2 bits, while their probabilities multiply as ½·½ — and only the logarithm turns multiplication into addition. Shannon's original paper gives an axiomatic derivation: any measure of information should be continuous, should increase monotonically as options multiply, and should be decomposable (splitting one choice into two stages must not change the total). He proved that −Σ p log p is the only form satisfying all three. The logarithm was not chosen for convenience; it was forced.

Worth pausing on: entropy here is two things at once. Before the reveal it is uncertainty; after the reveal it is information. So entropy and information are not opposites — they are two names for one number, viewed from either side of the moment of disclosure.

Then comes the harder claim. Shannon's source coding theorem proves that no lossless code can have an average length shorter than H bits. That is a floor, not a figure of speech. ZIP, PNG and FLAC all crowd up against it. Morse code stumbled onto the idea by intuition back in 1838 — the most frequent letter, E, gets the shortest possible symbol, a single dot, while a rare Q gets a long string. Each letter of English actually carries only about 1 bit, far below the log₂26 ≈ 4.7 bits you would need if all 26 letters were equally likely, and it is exactly that redundancy that lets you read a sentence with letters missing.

Now set the two formulas side by side. When W microstates are equally likely, Boltzmann's S = k log W and Shannon's H = −Σ p log p differ only by the conversion constant k. Physical entropy and information entropy are not an analogy; they are one mathematical object wearing two outfits. (Von Neumann is said to have advised Shannon to keep the name "entropy" on the grounds that nobody really knows what entropy is, so he would always have the advantage in an argument. This article is the consequence of that advice.)

4. Machine Learning: Entropy Becomes Something You Can Optimise

In the first two rooms entropy is observed and computed. In the third it becomes a target to minimise — the most practically consequential mutation in the concept's history.

Training a language model means predicting the next token given the previous N. The model emits a probability distribution q over the entire vocabulary (roughly 50k–150k tokens); the true answer is one specific token, which as a distribution p is one-hot — 1 in the correct position, 0 everywhere else. What measures the gap? Information theory answers with cross-entropy:

H(p, q) = −Σ p(x) log q(x) = H(p) + KL(p‖q)

Read it as: the average number of bits per symbol it costs to encode data that truly follows p using a code optimised for q. It splits cleanly. H(p) is the data's intrinsic entropy — a constant, independent of your model. KL(p‖q) is the penalty you pay for using the wrong distribution (the KL divergence, always ≥ 0, and zero exactly when q = p). Since H(p) is fixed, minimising cross-entropy is minimising KL, which is making the model's distribution approach the true one.

Because p is one-hot, the sum collapses to a single term: loss = −log q(correct token). Assign the right answer 0.99 confidence and the loss is about 0.01; assign it 0.001 and the loss is about 6.9. Confident and right earns a mild reward; confident and wrong takes a savage penalty.

These numbers carry a real scale. An untrained model on a 50k vocabulary sits at loss ≈ ln(50000) ≈ 10.8, which is exactly the uniform-guessing baseline. A well-trained LLM on web text runs about 2.0–2.5. The headline metric, perplexity — the exponential of cross-entropy — reads as "how many words is the model effectively torn between at each step," which works out to roughly 8–12. Raw UTF-8 text runs about 5 bits per byte, so a well-trained LLM compresses human text to 30–50% of its original size, comfortably beating gzip's roughly 70%.

This is the rigorous version of the "learning is compression" thesis: to predict the next token that accurately, a model has no choice but to internalise syntax, world knowledge and chains of reasoning. Kolmogorov complexity pushes the idea to its limit — an object's information content is the length of the shortest program that generates it — and the minimum description length principle picks models on that basis, with Occam's razor built in for free.

But the first hairline crack is already visible in this room, and it is written on the source page. Absolute loss values are not comparable across models: tokenizers with different vocabularies produce numbers on entirely different scales (GPT's BPE at around 50k versus Llama's SentencePiece at around 128k differ by a full bit at the baseline alone). Perplexity is meaningful only within a fixed tokenizer. A quantity with units, a formula and hard theory behind it loses comparability the moment you swap the coarse-graining convention. That is exactly the theme of §7 — except that here it is merely an engineering footnote. Outside this room, the same move becomes a con.

5. Life: Order Is Borrowed, and Repaid in Flow

The fourth room poses the oldest question. Life assembles scattered atoms into cells, order rises against the current — surely this violates the second law?

It does not, and the operative word is isolated. The second law governs the total entropy of isolated systems, and life is an open system: it continuously takes in low-entropy energy (sunlight, food) and expels high-entropy waste (heat, carbon dioxide, metabolic residue) back into the environment. So long as it exports more entropy than it removes from itself, the books balance.

And those books can be made very concrete. A seated adult radiates roughly 100 watts of heat — about the same as an old incandescent bulb. Your body temperature and the warmth of your breath are the entropy tax you pay the universe to keep your insides orderly. Scale up to the planet: Earth receives a small number of high-energy, low-entropy visible photons from the Sun and radiates back into space roughly twenty times as many low-energy infrared photons. Energy in and energy out are nearly equal; entropy rises sharply. That spread is what funds every scrap of local order on Earth — photosynthesis, evolution, the thought you are having now. On the full accounting, life is not an exception to rising entropy but an unusually efficient channel for producing it.

Dissipative Structures: Order Is a Verb

Prigogine's dissipative structures — ordered forms that exist only while a flow of energy passes through them — invert an old intuition entirely. Order does not mean equilibrium, stability, stillness. On the contrary: push a system far from equilibrium and order emerges spontaneously.

The cleanest demonstration is Bénard convection. Heat a thin layer of oil in a pan. While the temperature difference is small, heat travels upward by random molecular collisions and nothing interesting happens. Push the difference past a critical point and the entire layer abruptly organises itself into a tidy grid of hexagonal convection cells, as though someone had laid a honeycomb underneath. No external design whatsoever; a temperature gradient alone draws the pattern. A hurricane is the same principle at enormous scale — driven by the difference between sea surface and high altitude, and dispersing into turbulence within hours once the heat source is cut.

The corollary is sharp: structure is fundamentally a verb, not a noun. A whirlpool, a candle flame, a cell, a company — each exists only while energy streams through it, and dissolves when the stream stops. So stop chasing order that you "build once and it stays built," and instead build a metabolism that regenerates order continuously. A codebase tidied once and abandoned will rot; a codebase with an ongoing flow of refactoring stays orderly indefinitely.

This framing also supplies one excellent diagnostic question, which I think is the most valuable thing in the whole analogy: what energy flow sustains this order, and where is the entropy going? If you cannot see where the entropy is being exported, it is most likely accumulating quietly inside the system as hidden technical debt or hidden burnout. Distributed systems offer a nice corroboration: the mechanism Cassandra and Dynamo use to hold replicas consistent is called, in so many words, anti-entropy repair.

6. The Shared Spine: Where It Really Is One Bone

Now stack the first four rooms on top of each other. What they share is not a metaphor but a mathematical identity plus a physical law. The spine has three vertebrae, and every one of them is hard enough to run experiments on.

Vertebra one: counting states equals measuring uncertainty. When W microstates are equally likely, S = k log W and H = −Σ p log p are mathematically identical up to the constant k. Both count the same thing: how many microscopic possibilities remain hidden beneath the macroscopic appearance you can see. Viewed before the reveal that count is uncertainty; viewed after, it is information; viewed physically, it is entropy. Three names, one number.
Vertebra two: uncertainty equals code length. Shannon's source coding theorem promotes that number from a description to a floor — no lossless code averages fewer than H bits. Entropy thereby acquires an operational definition: if you want to know a distribution's entropy, compress it, and the limit you hit is the answer.
Vertebra three: bits equal joules. Landauer's principle states that erasing one bit of information must dissipate at least kT ln2 of heat into the environment. That welds abstract bits to scalding physical entropy — even forgetting has an electricity bill. It also resolves Maxwell's demon: the demon really can lower physical entropy using information, but it settles the account when it erases its own memory.

The machine-learning room sits directly on top of that spine with no discount required. Cross-entropy is denominated in bits (or nats); the loss value is a code length; perplexity is two raised to the entropy. When an engineer says loss fell from 2.5 to 2.3, the statement is a literal fact of information theory: each token now costs 0.2 bits less.

The life room sits on the spine too, with one extra step. The entropy accounting for a cell, for Earth, for any dissipative structure is genuine thermodynamic accounting, denominated in joules per kelvin, and measurable — those 100 watts, that twenty-fold count of infrared photons. It is not a figure of speech; it is bookkeeping.

Put differently: the spine running from "number of microstates" to "measure of uncertainty" genuinely connects physics, information, machine learning and bioenergetics. Four rooms, one machine. This is not what the article sets out to demolish — it is what the article sets out to protect.

7. The Break: What Has to Be True Before It Earns the Name

Which brings us to the fifth room, and the whole neighbourhood built around it: rooms get messy on their own, organisations suffer entropy, knowledge systems decay, relationships need work or entropy takes them.

Every one of those claims is intuitively true. Rooms do get messy; organisations do go slack. The problem is not the conclusion but what is holding it up. What these usages borrow is the sense of inevitability the phrase carries: this is a law of physics, the universe is built this way, resistance is not on the menu. And that inevitability was earned by everything in the preceding six sections — countable microstates, a declared coarse-graining, a number with units, the magnitude 10²³, and a law that emerges from large numbers. Strip all of that away and "entropy increases" is nothing but a tone of voice.

So the test should not be the vague question of whether something is "physics or metaphor." Here are four criteria you can check one by one.

Test one: can you say what a microstate is, and which coarse-graining you are bundling them under? This is step one of computing an entropy, not an afterthought. For a cup of water the microstate is every molecule's position and velocity and the macroscopic variables are temperature and volume — both specified, so W is countable and S is computable. What is a microstate of a room? The position of every dust particle? Then the room's entropy soars regardless of whether you tidy it. The location of each object? Then you must first define what counts as an object — a book counts, but does each page? Choose a different coarse-graining and the answer changes by arbitrary factors. Any usage that cannot answer "what am I counting" cannot write down W, and therefore cannot write down S.
Test two: does the number have units, and can you report its value at two different times? Thermodynamic entropy is in joules per kelvin, information entropy in bits, cross-entropy in bits per token. You can say this text has an entropy of 4.7 bits per character, or that after three days of training the loss went from 2.5 to 2.3. So what was your organisation's entropy last quarter? Nobody can report that number, and nobody actually wants to — because the moment a figure is demanded, the claim empties out. A quantity that only ever appears in the comparative and never as a value is not a quantity.
Test three: does the "law" emerge from large numbers? The second law is hard because of the magnitude 10²³ — disordered arrangements outnumber ordered ones so overwhelmingly that random evolution slides into them essentially without fail, and the recurrence time is too long for the universe to contain. How many "particles" are in a room? Dozens of objects, maybe hundreds. At that magnitude the overwhelming-majority argument has no numbers behind it. Rooms do get messy, but not by the law of large numbers — by a biased random walk: you take things out more often than you put them back. That is a behavioural explanation, not a thermodynamic one, and it makes a completely different prediction — hire someone to tidy daily and the room stops getting messy. A "law" that people overturn every day at trivial cost is no relative of the second law. The defining feature of the real second law is that you cannot build any machine that beats it — which is precisely why the second class of perpetual motion machine is a dead end — not that things get worse when neglected.
Test four: are you treating an open system as an isolated one? The second law governs isolated systems. Every object in the social analogies — a team, a codebase, a marriage, a body of knowledge — is without exception an open system, with energy, information and people crossing its boundary daily. Applying "the entropy of an isolated system never decreases" to an open system swaps out the law's precondition. The reason the life section holds up is exactly that it does the open-system accounting honestly: so much low entropy in, so much high entropy out. Anyone who skips that ledger and keeps the conclusion has skipped the only difficult step.

Running the Four Tests on Room Five

Apply them to psychic entropy. Csikszentmihalyi says openly in Flow that he borrowed the word from physics: the default state of consciousness trends toward disorder, since doing nothing does not quiet the mind but fills it with unresolved worry. Anxiety is entropy (challenge overwhelms skill) and boredom is entropy too (skill has nowhere to go); flow is the opposite, attention all pointing one way with intrusive thoughts squeezed out.

Score it. Microstates? None. Units? None — you cannot report that this afternoon's psychic entropy was 3.2. Large numbers? No. Open system treated as isolated? Partly. All four fail.

But notice that the conclusion is not "psychic entropy is nonsense." It is a naming, and a good one: it files anxiety and boredom — two apparently opposite forms of suffering — under a single category, as mismatches between attention and goals. That is a genuine conceptual unification and it is the book's contribution. What it borrows is entropy's shape (default drift toward disorder, order requires sustained investment), not entropy's mathematics. The problem was never the borrowing; it is failing to return — refusing to admit that what was borrowed is a shape, and then invoking the authority of a physical law to underwrite a psychological claim. Flow itself is comparatively honest about this, and the source page records where the book deserves a heavier discount anyway: the experience sampling method rests entirely on self-report, is hard to falsify, and physiological stories like "reduced prefrontal activity during flow" remain more conjecture than finding.

The dissipative-structure analogy scores considerably better and deserves separate treatment. Calling a codebase a dissipative structure honours the spirit of test four — it explicitly treats the object as an open system and demands you answer where the energy comes from and where the entropy goes. It is a structural analogy (an ordered form must be sustained by continuous flow and collapses when the flow stops), and that structure has real content and can be checked: you can genuinely go and identify the flow, and see what happens when it stops. It still yields no number (tests one and two fail), which makes it a good model rather than a measurement. That distinction is worth defending.

The Test in One Sentence

Thermodynamic entropy is not "mess" — it is the logarithm of a count. Any usage that cannot answer what am I counting, how am I bundling, and in what units, has borrowed not entropy's content but entropy's prestige.

8. When the Analogy Is a Tool, and When It Is Only Rhetoric

Demolition without a replacement is just a different rhetoric. Three tiers, hardest first.

Tier one: when it is literal mathematics, actually compute it

Whenever you have a probability distribution in hand, entropy is not a metaphor but a number you can put through a calculator. The mental models site offers an excellent practical test: to judge whether a piece of information has value, ask how much it shrank your uncertainty about something. If your internal probability distribution has not budged after reading a report, that report's information content was zero, however long it ran. A two-hour meeting that leaves your judgement about the decision unchanged contributed zero entropy — it was pure ritual.

Its sibling concept, mutual information (I(X;Y) = H(Y) − H(Y|X), how much knowing X reduces your uncertainty about Y), is even more useful at this tier: a metric's value equals its mutual information with the outcome you actually care about. KPIs fail — Goodhart's law — precisely because optimising the proxy destroys the shared information between proxy and goal with your own hands. At this tier, information theory is not inspiration. It is an instrument.

Tier two: when it is a structural analogy, name the whole mechanism

Dissipative structures are the model case. The analogy can be used honestly, on condition that you name all three components: which flow sustains this order, where does the energy come from, and where is the entropy going? If you cannot answer the third, you are not drawing an analogy — you are borrowing gravitas. The physics-thinking page's audit is exactly this: what energy flow sustains this order, and where does the entropy go? Answer both and the analogy is doing work. Fail to, and it is just a coat of physics-coloured paint on the platitude that things need ongoing investment.

Worth noticing: analogies at this tier can usually be restated without the word "entropy" at all, losing nothing. That translatability is the evidence that they are doing work — it shows what you borrowed was a mechanism. Conversely, if a sentence has nothing left once you delete the word "entropy," then the word was all it ever had.

Tier three: when it supplies only inevitability, it is rhetoric

The tell is simple: look at what the word is doing in the sentence. If it is doing explanatory work — saying why this happens, predicting what comes next, pointing at where to intervene — it is earning its place. If it is doing exculpatory or fatalistic work — organisations bloat, that's entropy, nothing to be done — it is a shortcut, and the thing being shortcut is physics's authority. The genuine second law is never used to excuse anything. It delivers relentlessly specific and actionable conclusions: a refrigerator can cool its interior, and the price is your electricity bill; the Carnot limit gives every engine a target it can never reach but is worth approaching; stop trying to build a perpetual motion machine. A real law tells you what the price is. It does not tell you to stop struggling.

Finally, back to the five rooms. The connection between them is real: physical and information entropy are one mathematical object, Landauer's principle makes bits and joules convertible, and life's entropy ledger can be tallied down to the watt. That genuine unity is the entire reason the word is valuable. And being valuable is exactly what makes it worth stealing.

So the next time you hear that something is entropy, don't ask whether the claim is right. Ask three questions: What are you counting? How are you bundling? In what units? Answer all three and you have physics. Fail them but explain the mechanism cleanly and you have a model. Fail all three and still draw conclusions from it, and you have rhetoric — worth hearing, not worth deciding on.
